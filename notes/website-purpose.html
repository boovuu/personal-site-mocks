A working guide

What the F do you want your website to do?

A working guide to purpose, buyers, evidence, responsibility, and the brief.

The first decision is what the website is expected to change for the business. A redesign, a new platform, and a stronger visual identity are possible responses. They need a reason to exist.

My work across publishing, campaigns, measurement, and the systems behind them has shaped the questions I ask before a website brief. This guide brings those questions together with research on brand effects, credibility, and buying decisions.

1. Name the business decision

Start with the commercial context. Are we entering a market, explaining a changed offer, improving the quality of opportunities, supporting customers, or making a complex portfolio easier to understand? Choose a primary purpose and describe the secondary ones. The homepage cannot give equal priority to every internal request.

A useful statement is specific enough to reject work: “Help operations leaders assess our fit before requesting a demonstration.” It names a person and a decision. “Make the site better” does neither.

Write down what success would make possible, the period over which it matters, and what the website cannot resolve by itself. An unclear product proposition needs product and commercial decisions as well as copy.

2. Understand who must reach agreement

Describe the people involved in the purchase and their concerns. A marketing leader may care about business impact and time to market; finance about exposure and terms; a technical stakeholder about integration and responsibility. Investigate the actual buying group rather than assigning concerns by job title alone.

Use recent enquiries, sales conversations, customer interviews, and lost opportunities. Ask what people needed to understand before moving forward, which alternative they considered, and what made the decision difficult. Avoid turning internal assumptions into customer quotations.

The Edelman–LinkedIn 2025 research highlights hidden decision-makers as an audience for useful thinking. My application is to make material shareable inside a buying group. The survey does not prescribe a particular site structure.

3. Give the website a role in the buying journey

What does the buyer already know on arrival? A referral, a search visit, and an active campaign may bring different levels of familiarity. Consider how each visitor can orient themselves without building a separate website for every route.

Choose the useful progression: understand the offer, inspect a relevant example, compare options, ask a question, book a demonstration, or complete a purchase. Contact is one possible step. For a buyer still exploring, a well-answered question may be more appropriate.

Binet and Field distinguish longer-term brand effects from short-term response. The IPA summary supports evaluating those time horizons separately. Applying that distinction to a website’s roles is my interpretation; there is no fixed budget ratio in this guide.

4. Decide what you can credibly show

List the claims the buyer will encounter and the evidence available for each: an inspectable artifact, a documented result, a customer account, a process explanation, or a clear boundary. Separate what you owned from what the wider team achieved. Give numbers a baseline, a period, and a definition.

If client material cannot be disclosed, use an agreed redaction or a smaller public example. Anonymity protects the relationship; it does not make an unsupported claim stronger. Remove the claim if the available evidence cannot carry it.

NN/g’s credibility research connects trust with presentation, disclosure, accurate information, and wider web connections. The evidence register below is my proposed practice, not its tested framework.

5. Make responsibility explicit

Identify the person accountable for the purpose, who approves the proposition, who owns evidence, and who can make delivery decisions. Agree on the points where a commercial or legal issue should return to leadership. Give specialists authority within clear boundaries.

Then identify dependencies. A campaign may need a product decision, approved evidence, sales readiness, and enough capacity before a date becomes realistic. Resolve those questions early enough to change the plan.

My quartermaster essay develops this organisational analogy from Leeson’s historical research. It is a way to discuss responsibility, not a claim that pirate governance proves a modern management method.

6. Plan for the second team and the next market

Ask what must remain consistent and what should vary. A common publishing model can support different markets while preserving an agreed proposition and evidence. A shared system should make local judgement possible within useful limits.

Decide who maintains information, which changes need review, how obsolete material is retired, and what the next team needs to understand. Estimate the continuing cost of the system, including the people who operate it. The launch is one moment in its life.

7. Agree on evidence before judging performance

Record the present position before changing it. Define a qualified enquiry with sales, understand the current response process, and distinguish commercial outcomes from diagnostic signals such as clicks. A dashboard is useful when it informs a decision.

Use both observation and explanation: what people did, what they understood, why they hesitated, and what happened after contact. Where sample size permits, test a defined hypothesis. Where it does not, report the limits of the evidence rather than presenting a directional change as certainty.

For example, “More enquiries” is incomplete. Did suitability improve? Did the sales team respond? Did the opportunities progress? A website can contribute to the outcome without being its sole cause.

8. Write the brief the team can use

A one-page decision brief

  1. Purpose: the business change and why it matters now.
  2. Audience: the people, their decision, and the concern to resolve.
  3. Role: what the website contributes and what sits elsewhere.
  4. Proposition: the offer and the reason it is relevant.
  5. Evidence: supported claims, permissions, and limits.
  6. Progression: the useful next steps at different levels of readiness.
  7. Responsibility: decision owners, capacity, and dependencies.
  8. Continuity: maintenance, reuse, and future markets.
  9. Evaluation: the baseline, measures, and review date.
  10. Boundaries: budget, time, and deliberate exclusions.

For a company entering a new market, that brief may lead to clearer category explanation and local evidence before an enquiry form. For an established offer with high familiarity, the priority may be easier evaluation and a better route into sales. These are illustrative choices, not client case studies.

Use the answer

Bring the brief to the people who will approve and deliver the work. Where their answers differ, settle the decision before treating it as a design problem. Then judge proposed pages, content, and technology against the agreed purpose.

Research and scope

This guide combines the author’s stated website responsibilities with the primary sources linked above: IPA / Binet–Field on time horizons, Edelman–LinkedIn on buying-group influence, NN/g on perceived credibility, and Leeson on historical organisation. The brief, questions, and examples are editorial recommendations derived from that context. They are not measured client results.
