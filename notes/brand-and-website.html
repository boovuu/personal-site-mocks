Notes on direction

When does the brand sell? When does the website?

The distinction is useful when it changes a decision: what to invest in, what to ask the website to do, and what evidence would tell us it is working.

A buyer can arrive already inclined to trust a company. Another can arrive with a serious need and no familiarity with the name. The same website meets both, but it does not begin from the same position.

I use brand and website as connected parts of the buying environment. Brand can create recognition and preference before a visit; the website can make the proposition understandable, the evidence available, and the next step proportionate to the buyer’s readiness. The website also contributes to the brand through the experience it provides.

Different effects, different clocks

Binet and Field’s work distinguishes short-term response activity from longer-term brand-building effects and warns about evaluating marketing primarily through short-term online measures. The IPA’s summary of The Long and the Short of It describes that central distinction. It does not establish a universal allocation for every business, or a website-specific formula.

My implication for a website brief is to identify which effect we are asking for. A page intended to help an existing prospect assess a proposal has a different job from an essay intended to introduce a way of thinking to future buyers.

What the website contributes

At the point of evaluation, I want a buyer to understand who the offer is for, what changes, what the evidence supports, and what proceeding involves. The experience itself also communicates care.

NN/g’s usability research identifies professional presentation, disclosure, current information, and external connections as contributors to perceived credibility. Its study concerns website trust; it does not demonstrate a particular revenue lift or prove causality for a B2B sales cycle.

The buyer you do not meet

A website’s material can travel inside an organisation. The person making contact may need to explain the choice to colleagues who never speak to sales. The 2025 Edelman–LinkedIn report examines visible and hidden decision-makers and the influence of substantive thought leadership. It is survey evidence, not an experiment establishing that an individual article causes a purchase.

I take that as a reason to make the argument usable: explain the trade-off, show the basis for a claim, and give someone enough context to share it without a presentation from the author.

Ask where the difficulty begins

Observed difficultyQuestion to investigate
Few suitable people recognise the businessAre we reaching the right market with a memorable proposition?
People arrive but cannot explain the offerDoes the site make the audience, value, and evidence clear?
Interest does not become a qualified conversationIs the next step useful, and does it answer the buyer’s unresolved concern?
Conversations stall after contactWhat happens across price, product fit, internal agreement, and sales follow-through?

These are hypotheses to investigate. A low conversion rate alone does not identify the cause. Traffic composition, seasonality, product fit, and the response after enquiry can all change the result.

Measure the contribution

I would agree on separate evidence for recognition, evaluation, and commercial progression. Use buyer interviews and market measures alongside website behaviour; connect qualified enquiries to their later outcomes. Keep assisted effects visible where the evidence allows, and describe uncertainty where it does not.

The investment decision becomes clearer when the website has a defined role in the wider commercial plan.
